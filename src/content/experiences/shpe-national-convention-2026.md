Anaheim 2024 was my first SHPE National Convention. I was a sophomore, and at the time I was only studying Computer Engineering. Indianapolis 2026 was my last as an undergraduate. I'm now a senior, finishing degrees in both Computer Engineering and Computer Science.

In Anaheim, I was there to network and look for an internship for the following summer. This year I was looking for full-time roles, especially ones where hardware and software meet.

## From Anaheim to Indianapolis
In the two years between, I served as president of Miami University's SHPE chapter from August 2025 to May 2026, attended the SHPE Presidents Summit in Chicago, and spent the summer of 2026 as a software co-op at RoviSys. <!-- Link the Presidents Summit and RoviSys pages here once they're published. -->

This year I attended as a general member. Handing the presidency to a junior is a chapter tradition: the outgoing president stays involved for one more year to answer questions while the new president leads.

## Building Flappy Kiro
Amazon's "Game On: Build Flappy Kiro with Kiro Express" session was my first time using Kiro, Amazon's AI coding tool. I installed both the Kiro IDE and the Kiro CLI on my Arch-based laptop beforehand, so setup wouldn't eat into the session. The IDE is built on VS Code and imported my existing settings and extensions, so I was ready in minutes.

Seyha Kry, a Senior Technical Account Manager at AWS, led us through building a Flappy Bird-style game with Kiro. Most convention sessions are a talk followed by questions. This one had us building the whole time. We didn't get through the full demo in the time we had, but following Seyha and the online instructions, we got as far as Kiro's spec stage: the requirements, design, and task list for the game.

Everyone left with Kiro credits and an invitation to Kiroween 2026, Kiro's hackathon. Two friends from Miami and I have since registered to participate.

## Two Hospitality Suites
The Kiro workshop came with an invitation to Amazon's hospitality suite that evening. Groups rotated through a room of tables, each staffed by employees with their roles listed, so you could find the people closest to your interests.

I spent most of my time with a hardware engineer on Amazon Leo, Amazon's satellite internet network, who works on optical communications. He studied mechanical engineering and picked up electronics and fiber optics on the job. When I asked about roles that mix hardware and software, like FPGA, ASIC, or firmware engineering, he said those exist on Leo and at Annapurna Labs, and that Amazon supports people who want to grow into new areas.

The next day, I attended Texas Instruments' presentation, *Beyond the CHIP: How TI Moves Industries*, where three TI engineers talked about their work. One of them works on the amplifiers that power LIDAR systems, and he compared LIDAR with optical imaging for self-driving cars. Afterward, I talked with him about my senior design project, [3D Gaussian Splatting for Radar Imaging](/projects/radar-gaussian-splatting). A 4D extension of the same approach applies to the kind of imaging autonomous vehicles need.

He offered to look into TI's radar team before the TI hospitality suite that night. When we met again there, he had checked: no one from the radar team was at the convention, but he pointed me to a recruiter who works closely with them. He also pointed me to a data engineer who knows people on the teams behind TI's career accelerator roles.

## The Career Fair and the AWS Jam
Amazon's booth was the best career fair experience I've had. The lines were short, employees walked the line to talk with students while they waited, and staff helped match people with the engineers closest to their interests. I later learned that everyone working the booth had volunteered to be there, and it showed.

The software engineer I talked with went through my resume in detail and gave me Amazon-specific feedback on it. He also talked about how much flexibility Amazon gives people to move between teams, encouraged me to look at roles across the company, and pointed me to the AWS Jam.

The AWS Jam ran in the Innovation Lab next to the booth: a gamified set of hands-on AWS challenges built for teams of up to three. I competed solo under Miami University's name and finished 3rd. It was more hands-on than I expected, and 3rd place came with an AWS Jam backpack, shirt, and hat.

At the NVIDIA booth, conversations were quick five-minute slots. I talked with a DevOps engineer who builds CI/CD pipelines and still needs to understand the underlying hardware to do it well. It was another reminder that the line between hardware and software work is blurrier in practice than in job titles.

## A Talk That Stuck With Me
Yi Jia, a Senior Software Engineer at Bloomberg, presented "The Unfinished Career: A Da Vinci-Inspired Strategy for Non-Linear Growth." She walked through a 15-year path: leaving an AI Ph.D. program, leading enterprise ML systems, stepping away at a career peak to advise startups, and coming back to build semantic technology at Bloomberg. It was a good reminder that a career doesn't need to follow a straight line to end up somewhere meaningful.

## What I Took Away
**Learning on the job is normal.** Nearly every engineer I talked to described picking up a field they hadn't studied, from fiber optics to the hardware behind CI/CD. I don't need to match a job description perfectly to be a good fit for it.

**The hardware–software overlap is a real job category.** FPGA, firmware, and SoC software roles exist across more teams at these companies than I expected.

**Workshops and talks open doors.** The Kiro workshop led to the Amazon hospitality suite, and the TI presentation led to my conversations at the TI suite. The sessions where I showed up and asked questions were where I got the most one-on-one time with engineers.
